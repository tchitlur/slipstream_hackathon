Slipstream: a readiness atlas for rare-disease communities

Team tnt (Tanay Chitlur, solo). Hack-Nation Challenge 05, AI Atlas for the World's Rare Diseases. Live: slipstreamhackathon.vercel.app. Code: github.com/tchitlur/slipstream_hackathon

1. Challenge tackled

Problem. A parent who leads a small patient group for a monogenic epilepsy cannot easily learn which other disease community is further along the same biological road, what already exists for them, or what could safely be borrowed. "Similar" is treated as one thing when it is two: symptom overlap governs what registry design and outcome measures can be shared; mechanism direction governs whether a treatment strategy can be shared at all. User. Patient-group leaders first; clinicians and funders who advise them second. Built. A Next.js atlas over a TypeScript pipeline: an eight-rung readiness ladder per condition from public records, a "road" per condition (how the gene breaks), neighbors ranked by phenotype similarity, seven typed transfer rules producing borrow / do-not-borrow verdicts with counter-reasons, a "what to do this week" box, and a footnoted one-page letter to the community ahead.

Gene2Phenotype (curated mechanism)→HPO phenotypes → simGIC similarity→PubMed, ClinicalTrials.gov, NIH RePORTER, org sites→ladders + 19,992 verdicts→static JSON → app

2. Tools and models used

DataGene2Phenotype DD panel (2,866 records), HPO annotations, ClinicalTrials.gov v2 API, NIH RePORTER, PubMed E-utilities, FDA labels, organization and registry sites.
OpenAI gpt-5.4T1 mechanism claims from abstracts; T4 the letter; T5 review of claims that disagree with the curated mechanism; T6 three-level target label for trials and therapies. All structured outputs, content-addressed cache, priced ledger.
gpt-5.4-miniT2 trial classification; T3 disease-name reconciliation.
ComputationTypeScript pipeline (tsx, zod schemas shared with the app), graphology Louvain clustering and ForceAtlas2 map, MiniSearch client search.
AppNext.js 16 App Router, React 19, Tailwind 4, static generation from committed JSON, Vercel; vitest and a data validator run in npm run check.

3. What worked well

  • Evidence discipline held. Four evidence kinds (curated, extracted, computed, hypothesis) never mix; 1,230 model-extracted quotes verified as verbatim substrings with stored offsets, 51 discarded.
  • Verdicts are computed, not generated. 2,856 pairs, 19,992 verdicts, each with its strongest counter-reason; the validator proves every claim cites existing evidence.
  • Mechanism direction matters in practice. 13 trials exclude a variant class in their eligibility text; those quotes appear inline on the borrow cards.
  • Grounded letters. Four pre-generated letters, 101 sentences, 3 dropped by the grounding check.

4. What was challenging

  • Mechanism is contested. Published claims disagreed with the curated direction for many genes. The final T5 rule keeps patient-variant evidence (mixed cohorts, functional characterization) and rejects manipulations, other genes, therapy mechanisms and dosage contexts: 4 contested, 9 "both directions reported", 31 rejected and kept for audit. A human-override file is in place.
  • Common symptoms swamp similarity. A low-information share test fired on every pair until the median was reweighted by annotation frequency.
  • A price-table bug tripled the spend estimate and stalled strong-model work until the ledger was rebuilt from the cache.
  • Gene-level data is not condition-level. Trial and baseline matches were tightened from gene symbol to disease name.

Solo build; times from commit timestamps (UTC), Oct 3 to Oct 4.

  • 15:00 to 19:30: read the challenge, write the spec, decide the evidence model and the "two kinds of similar" idea, scaffold, probe every source.
  • 19:30 to 21:00: vertical slice on ten genes, then all stages: literature and cross-check, trials, reconciliation, organizations, funding, ladders, verdicts, map, atlas-wide layer.
